# Independent audit of the Platt comparison

The 628,472–629,001 core-hour shorter-schedule estimate is already 1.5712–1.5725 times Platt's approximately 400,000 core-hours. That ratio compares a component forecast with an observed completed numerical verification on different hardware and a different mathematical domain. It is not a measured formal/native slowdown. The shorter-schedule subtotal leaves 170,999–171,528 core-hours to reach twice Platt's total; unpriced work may exceed that allowance. The subtotal is scenario-dependent and cannot be treated as a mathematical lower bound on every possible algorithm. [Current forecast](../price-check-20261120/README.md)

## What the two domains contain

Platt verified nonprincipal primitive character moduli q≤400,000 to height max(10^8/q, 200+A/q), with A=75,000,000 for even q and 37,500,000 for odd q. He reported 29,565,923,837 functions and about 38 trillion isolated zeros, excluding the auxiliary Turing zeros from that count. The approximately 400,000 core-hours were accumulated on SSE2 Intel/AMD machines. His separate central nonvanishing study extended to q≤2,000,000. Its 438,152 ambiguous double-precision central intervals are not a zero-bracketing repair count for the finite-GRH run. The published theorem is 9.1; the arXiv v1 theorem is 7.1. [Platt, published text](https://research-information.bris.ac.uk/files/67056136/platt_grh3.0.pdf) · [Platt, arXiv v1 §7](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1305.3087#page=14)

An independent integer-factorization census for q=2,...,400,000 reproduces Platt's reported count exactly. Including the trivial primitive character of conductor one would give 29,565,923,838. The current nonprincipal domain restricts odd conductors to 132,900 and even conductors to 265,800; only multiples of four contribute among the even conductors. It contains **4,351,723,951 primitive characters** on 132,899 active conductors, or **14.7187%** of the historical character count. Audit.py independently reconstructs the primitive count by local prime-power factors and compares the selected height rule to Platt's bound at every conductor. All main target heights fit; the largest ratio is 0.9436564 at q=265,800, and at q=10,000 the respective heights are 6,580 and 10,000. Building auxiliary windows to prove completeness is separate from coverage of these main targets. A historical per-conductor CPU breakdown is needed to obtain a comparable historical subtotal; the 14.7187% character fraction is not such a breakdown.

## Highest-leverage unknown for a completed certificate

The current timing covers successful pieces of the sign and Turing workflow. A defensible positive upper bound on the cost of **going from coarse samples to a successful count on every character** is still missing. A nonzero coarse sign does not exclude an unobserved pair of zeros between neighboring values. Even a plausible number of main-grid brackets can fail the strict Turing comparison when the auxiliary interval misses zeros. Platt describes both phenomena, including moving auxiliary windows and increasing precision. The campaign's full-height trial closes two inverse-character pairs at one conductor with a suitable auxiliary window and targeted extra probes. The four formal channel tests still consume externally supplied raw samples. Selection of four successful channels supplies no failure-frequency distribution for the full domain. [Historical repairs](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1305.3087#page=14) · [Full-height count](../full-height-count/README.md) · [Formal sign/count join](../grid-sign-join/README.md)

There is a quantitative feasibility condition even without a repair-rate model. Dividing the remaining 170,999–171,528 core-hours by the independently counted 4,351,723,951 nonprincipal characters gives **0.14146–0.14190 CPU seconds per character**, on average, for *all* omitted work combined. If every residual hour were spent on just the 18,477,108 small-family characters, the allowance would be 33.32–33.42 seconds per character. These are necessary average allowances for the stated 800,000-hour objective. They assume the existing subtotal's selected precision, implementation, and sharing already succeed. Any separately payable analytic seeds, coefficient transfer, admission, assembly and principal treatment consume part of that allowance. Shared work must be charged once at its actual lifetime. The four-channel initialization/export timing cannot be multiplied by the entire census as a lower bound, and a per-character mean allows substantial variation.

A priced repair bound needs either a proven fallback with bounded cost on all remaining channels or a full census recording each admitted surplus, unresolved region and successful fallback. An unstratified success sample cannot show termination and cannot justify a finite worst-case allowance. For measured progress, stratify complete end-to-end counts by conductor, main/auxiliary height and inverse-character pairing; record the failed first attempts and incremental cost to close them. The conditional repair/count uncertainty is the completion risk. The present evidence does not establish it as the largest positive number of CPU hours among unpriced components.

The historical comparison excludes downstream Goldbach proof assembly, principal-zeta work and finite binary/prime-ladder work absent from Platt's nonprincipal census. A first formal acceptance run also includes kernel proofs of source/analytic bindings and output assembly beyond interval arithmetic. A separate optional replay must be charged only if required.
