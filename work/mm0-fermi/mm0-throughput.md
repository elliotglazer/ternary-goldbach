# MM0 throughput model for a Goldbach certificate through 4·10^18

Research snapshot: 2026-11-10. Primary source pinned to `digama0/mm0` commit `0d414c0bfdaaeb7fea571895127abc1fa5a3d956`; selected source files are saved in `sources/`. No verifier benchmark was run in this task. The rates below are explicit engineering assumptions, not measured MM0 arithmetic performance. No set.mm compilation or verification comparison is used to set them.

## Recommended numbers for the parent Fermi estimate

For the efficient Boolean-word certificate design below, use **5·10^7 to 5·10^8 elementary verifier-work units per core-second**, with **2·10^8** as a central case. One work unit means an executed proof opcode, an executed unifier opcode, or one argument/dependency-check iteration—not one mathematical arithmetic operation. This supersedes an initially suggested conservative `2·10^7` central rate, which was not empirical and was too pessimistic as the primary model for small cached templates.

For an efficient **native, pre-expanded-template proof emitter**, use **50–1,000 MB/s of serialized proof output per producer core**, central **500 MB/s**, as an explicitly unmeasured sensitivity range. At 250 bytes/update this is 0.2–4 million updates/s, central 2 million: half the central checking time. Unlike the checker, this emitter does not replay unification; it patches known reference slots, computes native byte results, and writes a sequential stream. The central case allows about 2,000 CPU cycles/update on a 4 GHz core. It excludes certificate discovery/sieving. An earlier generic-producer model of 5·10^7 equivalent verifier units/s would instead cost four times checking; retain that as a slower implementation scenario, not the efficient target. Combined generation plus verification costs add; if pipelined on separate resources, wall time follows the slower stage plus I/O.

On a roughly 4 GHz core the checker range corresponds to 8–80 cycles per counted work unit, with 20 cycles centrally. This is plausible for cached references and short, predictable unification loops; it is optimistic for cache-missing, allocation-heavy or bound-variable-heavy terms. Keep the earlier **5·10^6–5·10^7** range as a stress case for those harder workloads. Neither range is a measurement. Calibration on the actual certificate is required before a procurement or elapsed-time claim.

| Operation on concrete values below 2^62 | Assumed expanded verifier work | Central checking time at 2·10^8/s |
|---|---:|---:|
| 64-bit AND/OR with cached byte proofs and one fused lifting lemma | 100–300 units | 0.5–1.5 μs |
| Addition/comparison with cached byte/carry tables and fused lifting | 200–1,000 units | 1–5 μs |
| Full 62×62-bit multiplication with cached byte products/carries | 2,000–30,000 units | 10–150 μs |
| Quotient/remainder certificate of similar widths | Approximately one multiplication, addition and comparison | Approximately 10–160 μs |

These operation counts are **model ranges for a custom efficient implementation**, not counts extracted from an existing Goldbach generator or the stock `norm_num` tactic. Eight bytes give a linear number of addition steps and 64 byte products before carry handling for schoolbook multiplication. Repeated squaring, primality certificates, and table membership may require many such operations. There is no justification for identifying one CPU multiply, one sieved integer, one checked prime, or one 64-bit word with one MMB opcode. The stock hex evaluator plausibly costs substantially more; a separate uncalibrated range of 10,000–200,000 units per multiplication is appropriate if its recursive rewriting is used unchanged.

For the fused Boolean design, budget about **150–400 serialized bytes per 64-bit update**, with roughly 250 bytes centrally. Combined with 200 units/update and the central checker rate this is **one million updates/s and about 250 MB/s/core**. The full assumption ranges permit roughly 0.17–5 million checked updates/s. These are derived scenario numbers, not observed rates. The central producer assumption supplies two million updates/s, or 500 MB/s at 250 bytes/update. Physical I/O or shared memory bandwidth can dominate many-core scaling. Use `time = work/rate` and separately `time ≥ bytes/I/O_bandwidth`.

## Why byte-table caching really works in MM0

For each binary Boolean operator there are 65,536 input-byte pairs. Prove each result once from the chosen PA definitions. AND and OR separately require 131,072 cached facts; a byte-complement table needs 256. At the beginning of a proof chunk, instantiate the required closed theorems once and save the resulting proof objects. Later a `Ref` to such a proof pushes its conclusion directly; it does not rerun that theorem's unifier or its original derivation. Shared byte numerals ensure the pointer identities match.

Define a natural-number `word8` constructor for the weighted sum of eight bytes, and prove a generic lifting theorem once. It takes 24 byte values (eight left, right and result bytes) and eight byte-operation facts, and proves the corresponding operation on the three `word8` terms. Arithmetic on those parameters is handled in the generic theorem's proof, which is not replayed at each application. Its use still checks its statement and hypotheses.

A representative application has about 24 argument references, eight cached proof references, roughly 5–15 commands to build/reference the conclusion, and one theorem command: approximately 40–50 proof commands. Its unifier inspects three word constructors and their 24 byte slots, plus eight small byte-equation patterns: approximately 70–100 unifier commands, plus 24 parameter checks and a small amount of term construction. This supports a central **about 200 work units/update**, with the table's 100–300 range allowing layout optimization and extra range bookkeeping. The central 250-byte estimate assumes mostly 5-byte references once heap indices are large. This is a source-guided hand count of a proposed template, not a compiled count.

The lifting theorem can avoid eight separate range hypotheses if it uses a defined low-byte extraction/coercion; alternatively cached byte-range facts or byte-result theorems carrying ranges add modest overhead. The design must be fixed before counting exactly. Special masks such as zero/all-ones and repeated words can use stronger symbolic lemmas and cost less. A whole-array identity may beat one update per word; this report does not impose the word trace as a lower bound.

Cache scope matters: the reference verifier resets proof storage at each theorem. A closed theorem remains globally callable, but a proof-object `Ref` cannot cross that boundary. Hoist tables again per chunk, or load only the byte pairs actually used. Raise the default 65,536 heap-slot limit enough for the table, expressions and working proof nodes, and limit chunk size by the store capacity. Several hundred thousand or a million updates per chunk can amortize table setup, subject to measured expression-store growth. This is a bounded-memory optimization problem, not unlimited global sharing.

## What the primary sources establish

The MMB specification encodes proof commands in 1–5 bytes. The proof and expression heaps support saved values and backreferences. Equality checks rely on shared expression identity; constructing an equal-looking expression twice does not automatically make it the same node. A producer should therefore hash-cons terms and deliberately emit Save/Ref operations. The format also has separate unification programs for theorem statements. These facts explain both the possible compactness and why bytes alone are not an operation count. [Pinned MMB specification](https://github.com/digama0/mm0/blob/0d414c0bfdaaeb7fea571895127abc1fa5a3d956/mm0-c/mmb.md)

The C implementation makes the extra work visible: `run_proof` starts at line 341; theorem application at lines 478–515 checks arguments and variable dependencies, then calls `run_unify`; that interpreter starts at line 213. A saved-reference step does much less work. At each theorem, lines 770–788 reset the expression store, check the proof, and check its final statement. There is no hardware-integer arithmetic oracle hidden in these loops. [Pinned verifier.c](https://github.com/digama0/mm0/blob/0d414c0bfdaaeb7fea571895127abc1fa5a3d956/mm0-c/verifier.c)

The reference executable memory-maps its MMB input and verifies it against a separately supplied MM0 specification. The documented normal invocation is `mm0-c proof.mmb < spec.mm0`. The parser must remain enabled for final acceptance: `NO_PARSER` does not establish that the proved theorem is the requested Goldbach statement. The README documents optimization using `-O2` and profile-guided optimization, but supplies no arithmetic-certificate throughput measurement usable here. [main.c](https://github.com/digama0/mm0/blob/0d414c0bfdaaeb7fea571895127abc1fa5a3d956/mm0-c/main.c), [mm0-c README](https://github.com/digama0/mm0/blob/0d414c0bfdaaeb7fea571895127abc1fa5a3d956/mm0-c/README.md)

## PA arithmetic is practical, but evaluation is not automatically a proof

The pinned `peano.mm1` already proves quotient/remainder facts, divisibility, gcd/Bezout facts, binary constructors `b0,b1`, four digit-addition rules (`addb00` through `addb11`, lines 3063–3066), comparisons, powers and shifts. Natural numbers also encode finite sets through bit membership, with shift and extensionality lemmas. This is enough foundational support to build packed-word certificate verification without representing an integer as 4·10^18 successor applications. It is not a ready Goldbach sieve verifier. No primality certificate theorem or high-throughput Boolean-word evaluation tactic was found in the inspected core files. [peano.mm1](https://github.com/digama0/mm0/blob/0d414c0bfdaaeb7fea571895127abc1fa5a3d956/examples/peano.mm1)

There is actual proof-producing arithmetic in `peano_hex.mm1`: `mkadd`, `mkmul` (from line 563), comparisons and `norm_num`. Division/modulo at lines 705–732 compute candidate q,r in the metaprogram, then prove `b*q+r=a` and `r<b`; they do not replay the division algorithm as a successor trace. Digit-product theorems are generated once and reused. This is the right pattern for certificate production. However, this file introduces hex/character/string sorts and axioms. If the final requirement is exactly the chosen PA axiom set, port the arithmetic to natural-number digit definitions or explicitly discharge the extension; do not silently count the extra axioms as PA. [peano_hex.mm1](https://github.com/digama0/mm0/blob/0d414c0bfdaaeb7fea571895127abc1fa5a3d956/examples/peano_hex.mm1)

Carneiro's dissertation expressly distinguishes ordinary `eval` from proof production: its native arithmetic evaluation is useful for finding a witness but does not prove the result. Its discussion of the hex library and MMC word operations supplies design precedent, not a measured speed or an automatic bridge from a successful native computation to the requested theorem. [Dissertation, §§2.2.1–2.2.3 and 3.2.7](https://digama0.github.io/mm0/thesis.pdf)

## Efficient production and sharing

1. **Prove a small generic certificate checker correct once.** Its inputs should be compact segment data, prime/sieve certificates, word-level coverage data, and interval endpoints. Its output theorem should assert every even number in the segment is a sum of two primes. This does not remove the need to prove that the concrete certificate passes the checker.
2. **Separate search from proof.** Run a native segmented sieve/search to discover certificates. The producer is untrusted and can use machine arithmetic freely; emit proof templates for the resulting equalities, inequalities, primality facts and coverage facts. Modular reduction should use a quotient witness as above.
3. **Share arithmetic structure aggressively.** Reuse digit tables, common moduli, powers, small-prime facts, word masks and expression tails within a segment. Cache proved primality or membership facts where many Goldbach witnesses use the same prime. Avoid re-proving the same prime for each even number.
4. **Prove coverage in blocks.** A proof of a mask identity or a symbolic interval/progression covering lemma can certify many even numbers. However, a native OR over 64 bits is not one free MM0 arithmetic step. Its concrete evaluation needs digit/word proofs unless covered by a stronger proved symbolic identity. The crucial algorithmic variable is the number and complexity of block certificates, not merely the number of even inputs.
5. **Use a purpose-built emitter after prototyping in MM1.** A native generator can instantiate predetermined proof DAGs without running general elaboration for every arithmetic step. Produce bounded chunks, retain exact statements and hashes, and emit a final checked coverage composition. Parser/elaborator timings should not be multiplied by the astronomical number of elementary cases.

### Format limits affect the architecture

The reference format uses 32-bit theorem/term references and pointers for statement tables. Statement data must fit in the first 4 GiB and each individual proof is bounded by its 32-bit next-statement displacement. The C verifier additionally has fixed stack, heap and store capacities, with a 64 MiB default store documented in the README; larger configured limits do not remove the format restrictions. Expression sharing is reset at a theorem boundary, while previously established theorem statements remain reusable. Thus neither one theorem per even number nor one unbounded giant theorem is a viable direct layout. [Format limits](https://github.com/digama0/mm0/blob/0d414c0bfdaaeb7fea571895127abc1fa5a3d956/mm0-c/README.md#limits)

Independent chunk verification needs an explicit trust/composition design. Re-importing a checked chunk's conclusion as a fresh axiom in the final file is not a single proof from PA. Options include bounded replay/composition within format limits, or a separately verified orchestration/certificate-checking layer whose theorem and execution evidence are part of the stated trust model. Merely hashing chunk files proves identity, not their mathematical conclusions. Raw `mm0-c` does not provide a cryptographic recursive-proof compression primitive.

## Consequences at the Goldbach scale

There are approximately `2·10^18` even inputs through `4·10^18`. At the revised efficient central checker rate, one verifier-work unit per even input alone would take about **317 core-years**. A 1,000-unit proof per even input would take about **317,000 core-years**, before generation and primality. These are scale checks, not a claim that an efficient certificate must contain a separate proof per even input.

One direct 64-way packed pass has `3.125·10^16` words. At 200 units/update and 2·10^8 units/s, checking alone is about **990 core-years**. Combining the 100–300 unit and 5·10^7–5·10^8 rate ranges gives about **198–5,942 core-years per pass**, before sieve initialization or prime justification. Central optimized production adds about **495 producer-core-years per pass**; the earlier slower generic-producer case would add about 3,961. A 250-byte/update stream would occupy about **7.8 exabytes per pass** if retained uncompressed. These are far better than the initial rewrite-based model, but still motivate symbolic/interval coverage compression and streamed verification. They must be multiplied by the actual number of passes/updates and divided by justified compression, not treated as the total Goldbach cost.

### Application to the team's small-bitmap / large-prime-anchor architecture

The zero-sum worker's proposed architecture uses about 512 large-prime anchors per block of `2^32` even inputs and a shared small-prime bitmap through approximately `2^33+D`. The parent reports about 15 selected masks per output word, rather than replaying all 512 anchors for every word. Conditional on those algorithmic counts, the mask layer has

`3.125·10^16 words * 15 = 4.6875·10^17 updates`.

At 200 units and 250 bytes/update, this is about **14,854 checking core-years** plus **7,427 production core-years** at the efficient central rates. Total uncompressed proof traffic is about **117 exabytes**, even if chunks are consumed and discarded rather than retained. Large-prime certification (about `2.38·10^11` proposed anchors), initial small-bitmap certification, final coverage assembly, and chunk replay are additional terms. This is not a claim that the complete architecture is already implemented or that 15 always suffices; those are algorithm-worker inputs to the throughput model.

The supplied access design is specific enough to avoid a hidden logarithmic lookup on every mask: certify the small bitmap in chunks of 256 or 1024 aligned words; scan output words in order; anchor offsets `k=(a−q−1)/2` lie below `2^19`, so accessed source words lie in an 8193-word sliding window. Unpack a chunk once with proved fixed selectors, save its `PrimeWord(index,value)` facts in the current proof heap, and then reuse references. In steady state about one new source word enters per output word, rather than one tree lookup per mask. Startup and proof-chunk resets still require loading the window again.

This access proposal needs an actual PA chunk-selector/cursor proof. MM0 itself does not make a huge encoded-list lookup constant time. The cache argument only makes the selector cost amortized, and the 8193-word claim depends on the algorithm's stated anchor-offset bound. Concrete index arithmetic must be shared/amortized as well; do not separately normalize a 62-bit index expression for every selected mask.

A selected mask can require an unaligned shift across two source words before OR-ing. Fusing this operation into one lifting lemma avoids an extra full intermediate-word proof. Cache byte-slice results for bit offsets 0 through 7 (at most `8*65536` byte-pair facts) plus OR facts. A plausible fused template consumes eight slice proofs, eight OR proofs and about 34 natural-number parameters. Its cost is closer to **200–400 work units/update**, rather than assuming every mask is a bare aligned OR. Add amortized chunk extraction/index costs per output word. Thus **200 is an optimistic efficient central design target; retain a factor of 2–3 sensitivity** for this full layer until a representative block is generated and checked. There is no intrinsic requirement to pay 64 separate PA bit proofs per update.

The enormous cumulative stream also requires the separately reviewed chunk/container/composition architecture; a single stock MMB file cannot simply contain an unlimited number of these chunks. A format extension or verified orchestration layer must preserve the final PA claim. Runtime arithmetic estimates do not discharge that proof-format obligation.

For any proposed compressed certificate architecture, use these variables:

- `K`: number of independent arithmetic/coverage certificate blocks;
- `e`: measured expanded verifier work per block;
- `b`: measured serialized bytes per block;
- `c`: number of checker cores, with parallel efficiency η;
- `p`: number of producer cores, with measured production bandwidth `g` in bytes/s (or directly measured blocks/s).

Then `verification_core_seconds = K*e/r_v`, `production_core_seconds = K*b/g`, `proof_bytes = K*b`, and idealized checking wall time is at least `K*e/(c*η*r_v)` and `K*b/I/O_bandwidth`. Add shared prelude and final coverage proofs, and account for prelude replay in every shard. Computing/searching the Goldbach certificate is another cost term, not included in `r_v` or `g`.

## Minimum calibration before tightening the estimate

Generate reproducible PA proofs for (i) 62-bit additions/comparisons, (ii) 62×62-bit products, (iii) quotient/remainder witnesses, (iv) actual proposed 64-bit mask operations, and (v) a complete representative coverage block, including primality/sieve evidence. Include 124-bit intermediates, realistic mask entropy, cold and warm data, and both shared and nonshared operands. Instrument counts of proof commands, executed unifier commands, argument checks, constructed expression nodes, heap/store high-water marks, and output bytes.

Measure producer CPU, verifier CPU, wall time, RSS and I/O separately. Preserve the exact PA specification and run the final acceptance with its parser enabled. The full coverage block is the decisive benchmark: excellent speed on repeated references or a small arithmetic library is not evidence of comparable speed on Goldbach certificates. Until that experiment exists, retain at least the tenfold throughput range and a separate order-of-magnitude uncertainty in work per block.
