# The finite Goldbach computation

The proof uses binary Goldbach through **564,386,325,504**. This covers 282,193,162,751 even integers starting at four. Lean 4.35 compiled the entire binary theorem on Linux. It needed the binary result only through **564,383,557,524** to prove the ternary statement. The extra 2,767,980 comes from finishing the last certificate interval. [Binary endpoint](https://github.com/elliotglazer/ternary-goldbach/blob/3ca2fcf7e3dd1897ff242d8e482aa7f82abe263b/ProductPrefix564386325504.lean) · [Finite theorem and Linux build receipt](https://github.com/elliotglazer/ternary-goldbach/blob/c6368decde84d4be04943117a2bfef991f9b1831/verification/community-grh/compact/finite-terminal-22f442fa/README.md)

## How the analytic proof determined the computation

For an odd integer $n$ in the upper part of the finite range, the RH argument finds an odd prime $p$ close enough below $n$ that $n-p$ is even and lies between four and the binary cutoff. A binary partition of $n-p$ supplies the other two primes. At height $H=2\times 10^{19}$, the Lean lemma requires an even bound $B\ge 126.2\sqrt{H}+2$. Lean bounds $\sqrt{H}$ by 4,472,135,955. Substitution gives 564,383,557,523, so the next even number suffices. A separate prime ladder handles the lower range. The final theorem combines the resulting finite statement with four certified groups of analytic inequalities. [Height reduction](https://github.com/elliotglazer/ternary-goldbach/blob/3ca2fcf7e3dd1897ff242d8e482aa7f82abe263b/FiniteAtHeight.lean) · [Cutoff arithmetic](https://github.com/elliotglazer/ternary-goldbach/blob/3ca2fcf7e3dd1897ff242d8e482aa7f82abe263b/LowerCutoff.lean) · [Assembly](https://github.com/elliotglazer/ternary-goldbach/blob/3ca2fcf7e3dd1897ff242d8e482aa7f82abe263b/CompletedFiniteRows.lean)

The historical binary Goldbach search went much farther. Oliveira e Silva’s project reported about 781.8 single-core years, or 6.85 million core-hours, for its work through $4\times 10^{18}$, including repeated checks and statistics on prime gaps and partitions. Our November 11 Lean pilot near that endpoint checked 64 consecutive even inputs using 12 prime anchors and 644 nodes in a shared primality certificate. It took 87.80 seconds elapsed and 126.03 CPU seconds to compile. Both measurements concern real work, but they cover different ranges, algorithms and output obligations. Lowering the binary cutoff removed the need to estimate the price of formally replaying the historical search. ## What the certificates prove

A November 20 generator built a sieved bitmap of source primes, packed it in 512-position chunks and used the same table for two adjacent blocks. It emitted Lean data and a balanced tree of Lucas primality certificates. The production checker also lets an external generator choose anchors and shifts. Lean checks the proposed data. The bitmap soundness theorem says that a set bit at position $j$ proves that $3+2j$ is prime. For each anchor, a shift of this bitmap marks the even numbers expressible as that anchor plus a source prime. The checker ORs those shifted bitmaps and tests whether the whole requested run consists of ones. A bitmap may omit primes. Acceptance still requires a witness at every position. [November 20 generator](https://github.com/elliotglazer/ternary-goldbach/blob/c6368decde84d4be04943117a2bfef991f9b1831/work/grh-goldbach-20261120/team/aws-pilot/wide/generate.py) · [Bitmap soundness](https://github.com/elliotglazer/ternary-goldbach/blob/3ca2fcf7e3dd1897ff242d8e482aa7f82abe263b/PrimeBitmap.lean) · [Row checking and coverage theorem](https://github.com/elliotglazer/ternary-goldbach/blob/3ca2fcf7e3dd1897ff242d8e482aa7f82abe263b/PrefixCheck.lean)

One row packs anchor offsets in 16-bit fields and supplies an inverse modulo the product of the anchors. The checker bounds each anchor between one and $(d+1)^2$, with strict inequalities, and verifies the modular inverse for a common integer $M$. The shared theorem $d!\mid M^e$ ensures every prime up to $d$ divides $M$. An anchor coprime to $M$ therefore has no possible prime divisor small enough to make it composite. The production basis uses $d=752{,}000$. The same basis and source bitmap serve many rows. At the final endpoint a single checker call covers eight rows of $2^{22}$ even inputs each. [Prime-product checker](https://github.com/elliotglazer/ternary-goldbach/blob/3ca2fcf7e3dd1897ff242d8e482aa7f82abe263b/PrimeProductCheck.lean) · [Shared basis](https://github.com/elliotglazer/ternary-goldbach/blob/3ca2fcf7e3dd1897ff242d8e482aa7f82abe263b/PilotPrimeProductBasis.lean) · [Last eight rows](https://github.com/elliotglazer/ternary-goldbach/blob/3ca2fcf7e3dd1897ff242d8e482aa7f82abe263b/ProductRange564319216642Interval.lean)

Earlier experiments tested which representations were worth formalizing. Shifted bitmap OR beat a packed-convolution alternative on the November native fixture. A sparse-cover experiment for a possible MM0 implementation timed the native coverage step alone. It excluded the search for witnesses, primality proofs and formal verification, so its extrapolation applies only to that operation. A later stock Lean experiment measured the cost of sharing. With a verified bitmap and prime-product certificate already loaded, adding a second interval of 33,554,432 evens and joining it to its neighbor cost 0.594–0.625 CPU seconds in three trials. A separate replay took 1.047 CPU seconds for the same phase. ## What stalled, and what fixed it

The first stall involved the two large prime bitmaps, `WideSource20` and `WideSource22`. Lean’s ordinary library build emits C as well as proof objects. The literal bitmap definitions were generating executable initializers even though the proof used only their logical values. Marking those private definitions `noncomputable` preserved the hexadecimal data and the certificates and allowed code generation to omit those initializers. The second stall involved two different files, `ProductRange141264937986Interval` and `ProductRange145559905282Interval`. Their concurrent Lean processes reached roughly 23–25 GiB resident apiece. Shared pages can appear in both process totals. The host was reclaiming memory while the interval proofs made little progress. [Bitmap change](https://github.com/elliotglazer/ternary-goldbach/commit/b6535e79fde64adc5288e02ad3393bd1eca94631) · [Interval scheduling change and measurements](https://github.com/elliotglazer/ternary-goldbach/blob/c6368decde84d4be04943117a2bfef991f9b1831/verification/community-grh/compact/finite-interval-scheduling/README.md)

Adding `set_option Elab.async false` to 103 interval files serialized the proof bodies **within each file**. Lake could still compile different files concurrently. Removing that one line reproduced the previous source bytes for every affected file. All 6,248 checker applications in those files subsequently compiled on Linux. The median module took 34 seconds, with a range of 5.1–65 seconds. The previously stalled 512-row and 320-row modules took 34 and 22 seconds respectively. On Windows, the 320-row module took 43.197 seconds elapsed and 41.625 CPU seconds, peaking at 1.70 GiB with C generation enabled. The package also groups finite targets into 98 Lake libraries and declares predecessor dependencies. Those dependencies govern scheduling across files. The source option governs work inside each Lean process. Each timing answers a different planning question. The 103 interval modules accumulated 55.01 minutes of overlapping module wall time within a 22 min 39 s build. A reservation of 32 vCPUs for that same duration would consume **12.08 reserved vCPU-hours**. Actual CPU-hours require summed process CPU times. A cold finite-source model using prior successful module timings and four simultaneous frontends gave about 94 minutes. Calibrating it against the measured 207-module warm build gave about 104 minutes. Both estimates assume similar per-module speeds and a populated Mathlib cache. The recorded 22-minute run directly establishes the warm time. The linked independent kernel receipts establish acceptance of their two stated binary endpoints. Measuring full-theorem export and both independent kernel checks requires its own end-to-end receipt.
