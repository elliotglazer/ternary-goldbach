# What the finite proof measurements cover

## The first block and its neighbors

On November 25 the 4.37.0-rc1 experiment exported the production theorem covering even naturals in `[6, 1258291206)`. The export held 20,337,444 bytes and 2,870 theorem declarations. With one CPU, generating it took 101.496 CPU seconds. Lean's checker used 143.294 CPU seconds and NanoDa used 121.815. Their peak resident memory was 1,777,964 and 1,147,184 KiB, respectively. This timing starts after building the source. [First and second block comparison](https://github.com/elliotglazer/ternary-goldbach/blob/c6368decde84d4be04943117a2bfef991f9b1831/verification/community-grh/compact/finite-first-two-replay.md)

Adding the immediately adjacent block extended the half-open range through `2516582406` exclusive. The conjunction added 5,141,476 export bytes, 902 theorem declarations, 101.820 Lean checker CPU seconds and 92.330 NanoDa CPU seconds. The paired export itself used *less* CPU, 83.161 seconds. Both exports passed both checkers with the three standard axioms. The result illustrates the uncertainty in assigning export cost by subtracting two runs on a shared machine. [Two-block measurements](https://github.com/elliotglazer/ternary-goldbach/blob/c6368decde84d4be04943117a2bfef991f9b1831/verification/community-grh/compact/finite-first-two-replay.md)

In the 4.35 experiment, an independent Mathlib statement started at 4 and used the inclusive even endpoint 1,258,291,204. The adapter discharged the lower endpoint and applied the first production block. The protected comparison and two checkers ran together for 262.71 wall seconds on one CPU. The two local versions of the note document the same run. One adds replay instructions about obtaining the pinned dependencies. [Inclusive-prefix experiment](https://github.com/elliotglazer/ternary-goldbach/blob/c6368decde84d4be04943117a2bfef991f9b1831/verification/community-grh/compact/binary-through-1258291204/README.md)

At 10,000,000,000, 19 production modules used 0.285 core-hours with a pinned Mathlib cache. Verification wrappers and exports used another 0.039 core-hours. NanoDa had accepted by 554.9 elapsed seconds in the first Linux invocation. The machine's memory guard stopped the subsequent Lean check. A second invocation on the identical read-only exports completed the structural comparison and Lean check in 620.8 seconds. Both validators accepted the same proof. These are separate runs and elapsed times include different phases. Later native Lean wrappers reached binary endpoints 21,408,506,880 and 21,945,377,792. Their small ternary corollaries apply the binary result to the odd input minus three. The independent dual-checker measurement pertains to the 10-billion prefix. [10-billion check](https://github.com/elliotglazer/ternary-goldbach/blob/c6368decde84d4be04943117a2bfef991f9b1831/verification/community-grh/compact/binary-through-10000000000/README.md) · [First larger native prefix](https://github.com/elliotglazer/ternary-goldbach/blob/c6368decde84d4be04943117a2bfef991f9b1831/verification/community-grh/compact/finite-21408506880/README.md) · [Second larger native prefix](https://github.com/elliotglazer/ternary-goldbach/blob/c6368decde84d4be04943117a2bfef991f9b1831/verification/community-grh/compact/finite-21945377792/README.md)

## Changing the factorial or the bitmap

The 415-module binary proof used 191,362 factorial certificate rows and 118,536 prime-product rows. The first 19,074 Power10 rows are already included in the factorial count. The two certificate families cover 50,164,265,470 and 232,028,897,280 even candidates. A 64-row experiment recomputed the inverses for a locally sufficient 5793! in place of 100000!. It held the interval, witnesses, offsets and checker fixed. Lean's and NanoDa's combined CPU cost fell from 99.300 to 66.874 seconds. Each arm paid for its own factorial. Blocks sharing a factorial across the full proof amortize that cost. The original early-block extrapolation gave a sensitivity range of 3.5–14 CPU hours for a limited finite replay proxy. It excluded exports and the analytic and GRH branches. [Factorial experiment and projection](https://github.com/elliotglazer/ternary-goldbach/blob/c6368decde84d4be04943117a2bfef991f9b1831/verification/community-grh/compact/finite-factorial-comparison.md)

A later experiment changed how Lean represents the same 4,197,491-bit integer. The original named a numeral with 1,263,571 decimal digits and proved that it equals a balanced expression. The alternate definition used the balanced OR of the same 193 terms and proved that equality by reflexivity. An independent reconstruction checked the entire integer. The interval proof and trusted statement stayed the same. The early handoff recorded a successful export with the variant checker still running. The later comparison records both stock kernel acceptances for both variants. One paused exporter spent 1,009.365 seconds suspended, so its gross elapsed time would give a misleading comparison. [Original terminal interval and receipts](https://github.com/elliotglazer/ternary-goldbach/tree/97a75b25d86ae187e955afebdf6751ed32764081/verification/community-grh/compact/binary-interval-564319216642-564386325504)

The one-tile theorem covers all 33,554,432 even inputs from 564,319,216,642 through 564,386,325,504. Its original 15-module source closure took 0.132 core-hours and its original verification wrappers and exports took 0.258 core-hours. The bitmap source itself cost 201.84 and 269.83 compiler CPU seconds in the two arms. The exports occupied 28,777,802 and 27,531,187 bytes. Each arm ran once and the reported Linux kernel CPU values are sampled lower bounds. [Original kernel comparison](https://github.com/elliotglazer/ternary-goldbach/blob/97a75b25d86ae187e955afebdf6751ed32764081/verification/community-grh/compact/binary-interval-564319216642-564386325504/receipt.json)

The 64-tile theorem instead covers 555,729,282,050 through 560,024,249,344 inclusive. The two variants yielded byte-identical consumer and private proof objects. Elaboration was averaged over A–B–B–A fresh builds. Both exports passed both Linux checkers on one CPU. The alternate representation saved 743 export CPU seconds and added 512 CPU seconds across the two kernels. The one-tile experiment had predicted the direction of the export change correctly and the direction of the kernel change incorrectly.

## The cost of one more tile

A stock Lean kernel experiment loaded the shared bitmap and primorial once, replayed the declarations for one production tile, then timed the additions for the next tile and their join. Three runs used 0.594, 0.625 and 0.594 CPU seconds for the second step. A separate portable replay used 1.047 seconds. Whichever representation a full proof uses, it still has to pay for the shared declarations, imports, export and both independent checkers. [Source and replay](https://github.com/elliotglazer/ternary-goldbach/tree/05cc905b770176d0586236433b07333312adf4d6/verification/community-grh/compact/paired-product-tile-kernel)

The paired representation test rebuilt the same augmented consumer and checked that both public and private objects were byte-identical. For the adjacent tile, the medians of the original and balanced trials were each 1.109 CPU seconds. Across two paired six-tile trials, the balanced expression cost 0.049–0.185 additional CPU seconds per tile, averaging 0.117. An initial trial stopped at a commit-memory guard before completion and was excluded. Seven distinct leaves from one module give little leverage for estimating the other 6,298 leaves of the 6,305-tile bitmap.
